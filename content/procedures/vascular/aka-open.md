## [aka-patho] Why above the knee, and when

**Mechanism.** The same disease as for a BKA: end-stage CLTI, sepsis, or trauma, but extending above the level where the calf can heal. A thigh amputation is chosen because it **heals most reliably**: the thigh has a richer blood supply (profunda femoris) than the calf, and muscle bulk covers the bone.

**Indications for an above-knee amputation (AKA)**

-   Ischemia or infection extending above a healable BKA level, or a BKA that has failed.
-   A **fixed knee flexion contracture**, or a patient who will not walk (bed-bound, advanced dementia, paralysis).
-   Trauma or infection of the leg around the knee; unresectable tumor of the lower leg; life-threatening sepsis (gas gangrene, necrotizing infection).
-   A patient too frail for a prosthesis: the aim becomes pain-free, healed, sitting.

**Contraindication.** Few, once other options are exhausted: only a patient who cannot survive anesthesia and surgery.

**Cost.** Losing the knee means a much higher energy cost of walking (about 65% more than a person without an amputation) and fewer patients ever walk again. Qaarie, Cureus 2023 (review of published series, very heterogeneous): 30-day mortality averaged about 16% after BKA and AKA alike (BKA 6.2–51.4%, AKA 12.7–21.7%); 1-year mortality about 28% after BKA and about 50% after AKA, partly because AKA patients are sicker; 5-year mortality 39–80%.

> **Evidence:** Myers & Chauvin, StatPearls Above-the-Knee Amputations 2023 (indications, few contraindications, 65% extra energy cost); Waters et al. 1976; Qaarie, Cureus 2023 (review of published series, very heterogeneous): 30-day mortality averaged about 16% after BKA and AKA alike (BKA 6.2–51.4%, AKA 12.7–21.7%); 1-year mortality about 28% after BKA and about 50% after AKA, partly because AKA patients are sicker; 5-year mortality 39–80%.

## [aka-case] Case: gangrene to the calf in a bed-bound patient

No chance of walking, a fixed knee contracture, and gangrene above the BKA level: an **above-knee amputation**. The aim: healed, pain-free, able to sit and transfer.

### Case

A **78-year-old woman**, bed-bound after a stroke 2 years ago; fixed knee flexion of 40°. Dry gangrene of the foot, now black skin to the mid-calf with a cold, painful leg. Femoral pulse palpable, no pulse below. Hypertensive, creatinine 140 μmol/L.

## [aka-incision] Mark the level and the fish-mouth flaps

Supine, a sandbag under the buttock. Measure from the knee joint line.

| Landmark | Measurement |
| --- | --- |
| **Femur cut** | about **12 cm above the knee joint line** (the level that leaves room for a prosthetic knee) |
| **Flap length** | each flap equals **half the diameter of the thigh at the bone cut, plus 1 cm** |
| **Flap design** | anterior and posterior (fish-mouth), or medial and lateral; equal, rounded flaps, no feathered edges |
| **Muscle** | divided about **2.5–5 cm (1–2 inches) distal to the bone cut**, to allow myodesis |

Cut decisively through skin, subcutaneous tissue and fascia; no undermining. In severe ischemia, no tourniquet. Keep as much length as will heal.

> **Evidence:** Myers & Chauvin, StatPearls 2023: femur cut 12 cm proximal to the joint line, muscle divided 1–2 inches beyond the bone cut; University of Washington transfemoral guide: flap length is half the limb diameter at the bone cut plus 1 cm.

## [aka-bone] Divide the muscle and the femur

Divide the quadriceps and hamstrings, **protecting the adductor muscle and its tendon** (needed for the myodesis). Elevate the periosteum to the level of the cut only. Divide the **femur 12 cm above the joint line** with an oscillating saw, bevel the sharp edge, rasp it smooth, and irrigate.

> **Evidence:** Myers & Chauvin, StatPearls 2023: femur 12 cm proximal to the joint line; University of Washington transfemoral guide: adductor muscle and tendon protected for myodesis.

### Question

**Q:** Why are the adductors protected when the muscles are divided?
- [x] They are needed to anchor the myodesis to the femur
  > An adductor myodesis keeps the femur centered and helps the stump function.
- [ ] They contain the nerve
- [ ] They are not important
- [ ] They make the flap thicker

## [aka-vessels] Femoral vessels and the sciatic nerve

In the adductor canal, identify the **superficial femoral artery and vein**: dissect, then **ligate (double tie, or tie and transfix) and divide** each separately before cutting. Identify the profunda femoris and keep it patent when possible: it is the stump's blood supply.

Pull the **sciatic nerve** and the femoral nerve branches down gently and **divide them sharply** so they retract; ligate any vessel that accompanies the sciatic nerve. Optional: a local anesthetic block of the nerve ends.

> **Evidence:** Myers & Chauvin, StatPearls 2023: femoral artery and vein dissected, ligated and divided; nerves sharply divided to reduce neuroma. Keeping the profunda patent and the nerve block are general technique, not from these sources.

## [aka-close] Myodesis, drain and closure

**Adductor myodesis**: with the hip in **5–10° of adduction**, anchor the adductor tendon to the femur through drill holes with non-absorbable suture. **Quadriceps myodesis**: with the hip in **full extension**, anchor the quadriceps to the femur. These stop the muscles retracting and the femur from drifting outward. Place a drain. Close the **fascia, then the skin** without tension; a padded dressing.

Common problems if skipped: muscle atrophy, wound infection, dehiscence, and flexion or abduction contractures at the hip.

> **Evidence:** Myers & Chauvin, StatPearls 2023: adductor myodesis with hip 5–10° adducted, quadriceps myodesis with hip in full extension; complications include atrophy, wound infection, dehiscence, flexion and abduction contractures.

### Question

**Q:** In what hip position is the adductor myodesis tied?
- [x] 5–10° of adduction
  > Tying in slight adduction keeps the femur centered; the quadriceps myodesis is tied in full extension.
- [ ] Full flexion
- [ ] 45° of abduction
- [ ] Any position

## [aka-after] After an AKA: hip, pain, mobilization, the other leg

**Wound.** Drain out when the output is low; inspect the flap; sutures out when healed (about 2 weeks in the BKA series cited; longer if ischemic). Failure to heal: debride or revise, rarely a hip disarticulation.

**Hip.** Prevent **hip flexion and abduction contractures**: no pillow under the stump, lie prone for part of the day, stump supported in extension when sitting, early physiotherapy.

**Pain.** Multimodal analgesia. Phantom pain is common (reported in up to 80% of patients in StatPearls; pooled 64% in a meta-analysis) and a more proximal amputation raises the risk. Treat early.

**Medical.** Glucose, nutrition, VTE prophylaxis, antiplatelet and statin for life, smoking cessation, depression screening (PTSD and depression are common), and daily inspection of the **other foot**.

**Mobility.** Early transfers and wheelchair training; a prosthesis only in a patient who can use one (it costs far more energy than a BKA prosthesis). Realistic goals, with the family, before surgery.

> **Evidence:** Myers & Chauvin, StatPearls 2023: phantom pain in up to 80%, contractures, depression and PTSD, 65% extra energy cost; Limakatso et al., PLOS ONE 2020: more proximal amputation is a risk factor for phantom pain; Qaarie, Cureus 2023: 1-year mortality after AKA about 50%.
