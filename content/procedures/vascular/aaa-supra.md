## [as-patho] Pathophysiology: abdominal aortic aneurysm

**What fails is the media.** Elastin is broken down (matrix metalloproteinases from macrophages and smooth muscle cells), smooth muscle cells die, and chronic inflammation involves the adventitia. The infrarenal aorta has the fewest vasa vasorum and the least elastin: most aneurysms start there. Risk factors: **age, male sex, smoking** (the strongest modifiable), family history, hypertension; diabetes is, oddly, protective.

Chain: Elastin degradation, loss of smooth muscle → Dilatation → Wall tension rises with radius (Laplace: T = P × r) → **More dilatation, faster growth** → **Rupture**

An aneurysm is a diameter of **3.0 cm or more**. Growth accelerates as it enlarges; rupture risk climbs steeply above 5.5 cm:

| Diameter | 1-year risk of (probable) rupture, untreated |
| --- | --- |
| 5.5–5.9 cm | 9.4% |
| 6.0–6.9 cm | 10.2% (19.1% at 6.5–6.9) |
| 7.0 cm or more | 32.5% |

**Repair thresholds**: 5.5 cm in men, 5.0 cm in women (smaller aortas rupture at smaller diameters), rapid growth (about 1 cm a year, confirmed), or symptoms (tenderness, back pain). Below that, **surveillance**: early repair of 4.0–5.5 cm aneurysms did not improve survival.

**The neck** decides the operation:

| Type (SVS 2021) | Where the aneurysm starts | Proximal clamp and repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Infrarenal** | a healthy neck of 10–15 mm or more below the lowest renal artery | infrarenal clamp; EVAR usually possible |
| Short neck | neck 4–10 mm | often suprarenal clamp; EVAR marginal |
| **Juxtarenal** | at the renal arteries (neck 4 mm or less), not involving them | suprarenal clamp, anastomosis at the renal origins; or fenestrated EVAR |
| **Pararenal / paravisceral** ("suprarenal") | involving the renal arteries, up to (pararenal) or including (paravisceral) the SMA | supracoeliac clamp, renal and visceral reconstruction; or fenestrated/branched EVAR |
| Thoracoabdominal extent IV | up to the coeliac axis or diaphragmatic hiatus | thoracoabdominal repair |

**In Africa** aortic aneurysms present younger and more often from **infection or HIV-associated vasculitis** (saccular, at atypical sites, sometimes multiple); many present ruptured. Always consider an infective (mycotic) aneurysm: fever, raised inflammatory markers, a saccular or lobulated shape, periaortic gas or fluid.

> **Evidence:** rupture rates: Lederle et al., JAMA 2002 (198 patients refusing or unfit for repair). Small aneurysms: UK Small Aneurysm Trial 12-year follow-up (Powell et al., Br J Surg 2007: no survival difference, HR 0.90) and ADAM (NEJM 2002: RR 1.21). Thresholds and surveillance: ESVS 2024 (Wanhainen et al.). Anatomical definitions: SVS reporting standards (Oderich et al., J Vasc Surg 2021). HIV-associated aneurysms: Nair et al. (J Vasc Surg 1999; mean age 30, often multiple, atypical sites). African AAA: Wu, J West Afr Coll Surg 2022.

**Normal sizes and when to act.** An aneurysm is a permanent dilatation to at least **1.5 times** the expected normal diameter.

| Artery | Normal adult (typical) | Aneurysmal | Usual threshold to repair |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ascending aorta | about 2.2–3.6 cm | dilated above 22 mm/m² (ESC) | 5.5 cm; 5.0 cm with risk factors, bicuspid root phenotype or Marfan; 4.5 cm if the aortic valve is being operated on |
| Descending thoracic | about 2.0–3.0 cm | dilated above 16 mm/m² (ESC) | TEVAR at 5.5 cm with suitable anatomy (ESC 2024); 6.0 cm (ACC/AHA 2022) |
| Infrarenal aorta | about 1.5 cm (women), 1.7 cm (men) over 50 | 3.0 cm or more | 5.5 cm (men), 5.0 cm (women); growth of about 1 cm a year; symptoms |
| Common iliac | about 1 cm | about 1.8–2.0 cm and more (definitions vary) | 4.0 cm (ESVS 2024, raised from 3.5 cm) |
| Popliteal | about 0.5–0.9 cm | over 1.5 cm or 1.5 × normal | 2.0 cm, or smaller with thrombus and embolism or poor run-off (SVS 2022) |

> **Evidence:** definition of aneurysm as 1.5 × normal: SVS/ISCVS reporting standards (Johnston et al., J Vasc Surg 1991); normal infrarenal diameters (AAFP 2006). Thoracic indexing and thresholds: ESC 2024 (Mazzolai et al.) and ACC/AHA 2022 (Isselbacher et al.). Iliac threshold: ESVS 2024 (Recommendation 135). Popliteal: SVS 2022 (Farber et al.). Normal ranges for the iliac and popliteal arteries are typical values, not guideline cut-offs.

### Question

**Q:** Why does an aneurysm grow faster as it gets bigger?
- [x] Wall tension rises with the radius (Laplace's law), so the larger the aneurysm, the greater the stress on an already weakened wall
  > T = P × r: at the same blood pressure a 6 cm aneurysm carries twice the wall tension of a 3 cm aorta. That is why growth and rupture risk accelerate with size.
- [ ] Blood flow slows
- [ ] Thrombus lining the sac weakens it
- [ ] Blood pressure rises with size

## [as-anatomy] The infrarenal aorta and its neighbours

From above down on the front of the aorta: the **coeliac trunk** (T12/L1), the **SMA** about 1 cm lower, the **renal arteries** (L1/L2, the right passing behind the IVC), the **left renal vein** crossing in front of the aorta just below the SMA (the upper limit of the infrarenal neck), the **IMA** from the left front at L3, and the bifurcation at L4.

What to protect: the **left renal vein** above the neck (and a retroaortic or circumaortic variant, which tears when clamping); the **duodenum** (3rd and 4th parts) over the aneurysm; the **IVC** to the right and the **iliac veins** behind the iliac arteries (the left common iliac vein runs behind the right common iliac artery): encircling the iliac arteries tears them; the **ureters** crossing the iliac bifurcations; the **superior hypogastric plexus** on the left of the aorta and over the left common iliac (dissect on the right to preserve ejaculation); the **lumbar arteries** behind, which back-bleed into the opened sac.

> **Note:** the reference CT ends at the 4th lumbar vertebra; the iliac and femoral vessels and the groins are drawn to typical adult dimensions below it.

### Question

**Q:** Which vein lies behind the right common iliac artery and is torn by careless encircling of that artery?
- [x] The left common iliac vein (near the confluence of the IVC)
  > The iliac veins lie behind and slightly to the right of the arteries; the left common iliac vein crosses behind the right common iliac artery to reach the IVC. Clamp the iliacs without encircling them.
- [ ] The left renal vein
- [ ] The inferior mesenteric vein
- [ ] The gonadal vein

## [as-case] Case: a pararenal aneurysm through the left flank

**The renal arteries arise from the aneurysm**: the proximal anastomosis must be above them, incorporating the visceral and renal origins. The **left retroperitoneal** approach gives the best access to the aorta above the renal arteries and up to the hiatus, and avoids a hostile abdomen (her previous laparotomy).

Alternatives: fenestrated or branched EVAR at high risk, where available.

> **Evidence:** Sicard et al. (J Vasc Surg 1995, randomised, 145 patients): the retroperitoneal approach had less ileus and shorter ICU stay; Cambria et al. (1990, 113 patients) found no important difference. ESVS 2024: the approach is chosen on patient factors and surgeon preference. Jongkind et al. 2010: renal dysfunction after complex open repair was common (median 18%).

### Case

A **66-year-old woman**, hypertensive, a previous laparotomy for a perforated duodenal ulcer. CT angiography: a **6.5 cm** aneurysm that **involves both renal artery origins** and reaches 1 cm below the SMA; the coeliac and SMA origins are healthy; eGFR 55.

### Question

**Q:** Why the left flank (retroperitoneal) approach here?
- [x] It exposes the aorta above the renal arteries up to the hiatus and avoids her scarred abdomen
  > The left retroperitoneal route gives direct access to the para- and supravisceral aorta (dividing the left crus) and stays out of the peritoneal cavity; the right renal artery is harder to reach from this side.
- [ ] It gives better access to the right iliac artery
- [ ] It is quicker for infrarenal aneurysms
- [ ] It avoids the need for a suprarenal clamp

## [as-flank] Left flank incision; the retroperitoneal plane

Right side down, the left shoulder rotated back about 60°, the pelvis flatter (the "corkscrew" position), the table broken at the flank. Incision from the tip of the **11th rib** posteriorly, obliquely to the lateral edge of the rectus sheath below the umbilicus; resect or enter the bed of the 11th rib. Divide the abdominal muscles; stay **outside the peritoneum**, sweeping it, the colon and the spleen forward. Take the **left kidney forward** with them (exposing the posterior aorta and the left renal artery) or leave it in place.

## [as-neck] Expose the neck and the visceral aorta

Through the left retroperitoneum: sweep the **left kidney** (or leave it posterior), the colon and the spleen forward; divide the **left crus of the diaphragm** to reach the supracoeliac aorta. The left renal artery is the landmark: follow it to the aorta. Expose the coeliac, SMA and both renal origins.

## [as-clamp] Heparin, clamp distally, then proximally

Heparin (about 70–100 U/kg). Clamp the **iliac arteries first** (so that debris from the sac goes nowhere), then the **supracoeliac** aorta at the hiatus: the liver, bowel and kidneys are all ischaemic; work fast, and **perfuse the kidneys with cold crystalloid**. Tell the anaesthetist before clamping: afterload rises; before releasing, fluid and vasoconstrictor ready.

> **Evidence:** ESVS 2024: consider cold renal perfusion when the suprarenal clamp time is expected to exceed about 25 minutes. In a systematic review of 1,256 open juxtarenal repairs (Jongkind et al., J Vasc Surg 2010), 30-day mortality was 2.9% and 3.3% needed new dialysis.

## [as-sac] Open the sac, clear the thrombus, stop the back-bleeding

Open the aneurysm longitudinally on its right anterior surface (away from the IMA), T-ing the incision at the neck and at the bifurcation. Scoop out the laminated **thrombus**. **Oversew the lumbar arteries** from inside with figure-of-eight 2-0 or 3-0 sutures; back-bleeding from the **IMA**: if it bleeds briskly (good collaterals) oversew its origin from inside; if the flow is poor and the colon may depend on it, keep a button for reimplantation.

> **Evidence:** ESVS 2024: routine IMA reimplantation is not recommended; consider it when pelvic and colonic perfusion is doubtful (both internal iliacs diseased or excluded, previous colectomy, poor back-bleeding). Colonic ischaemia after open repair presents with bloody diarrhoea, acidosis or distension: sigmoidoscopy early.

## [as-graft] Sew in the graft

**Bevelled proximal anastomosis**: the graft cut obliquely so that its suture line incorporates the **coeliac, SMA and right renal origins** as one patch (a Carrel patch), sewn with 3-0 polypropylene. The **left renal artery** is reimplanted as a button or bypassed with a side-arm. Move the clamp down onto the graft below the visceral origins in sequence: the viscera, then each kidney.

### Question

**Q:** Before tying the distal anastomosis, why flush the graft?
- [x] To wash out air, thrombus and debris that would otherwise embolise to the legs or pelvis
  > Brief release of the iliac, then aortic clamps flushes the graft; debris left in it embolises to the feet ("trash foot") or the pelvis.
- [ ] To test the proximal anastomosis only
- [ ] It is not needed
- [ ] To reduce heparin effect

## [as-renal] Cold renal perfusion; reimplant the left renal

With the supracoeliac clamp on and the sac opened, place **balloon perfusion catheters** into both renal ostia and infuse **cold crystalloid** (about 4 °C, commonly Ringer's lactate with mannitol, per unit protocol) intermittently; visceral ischaemia time is the other clock: keep it short.

After the bevelled proximal suture line, the **left renal artery** goes onto a side-arm or as a button reimplanted into the graft; restore flow to the viscera and right kidney first by moving the clamp down onto the graft.

> **Evidence:** ESVS 2024 (Rec 125): consider cold renal perfusion when suprarenal clamp time exceeds about 25 minutes. The perfusate temperature and composition vary by centre.

## [as-close] Release, close the sac over the graft, check the colon and the feet

Release the clamps **one leg at a time**, slowly, with the anaesthetist (declamping hypotension). Protamine if needed. **Close the sac over the graft** and then the posterior peritoneum, so the graft never touches the duodenum (aortoenteric fistula). Look at the **sigmoid colon** (pink, peristalsing, a mesenteric pulse) and the **feet** (pulses, colour) before closing.

Check both kidneys (colour, a pulse in each renal artery) and the bowel; urine output and lactate overnight.

### Question

**Q:** Why close the aneurysm sac and peritoneum over the graft?
- [x] To keep the graft away from the duodenum and prevent an aortoenteric fistula
  > Direct contact between the graft suture line and the duodenum can erode into the bowel months or years later: a catastrophic bleed.
- [ ] To stop back-bleeding
- [ ] To help the graft endothelialise
- [ ] For cosmetic reasons
