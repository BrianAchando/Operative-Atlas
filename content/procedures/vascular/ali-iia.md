## [iia-patho] Pathophysiology: the Rutherford grades and the clock

Acute limb ischemia is a sudden loss of perfusion (within 2 weeks) that threatens the leg. **Nerve and muscle tolerate severe ischemia for about 6 hours**; what separates the grades is whether **sensation and movement** are still there.

| Rutherford | Sensory loss | Weakness | Doppler (artery / vein) | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I viable | none | none | audible / audible | urgent imaging, revascularize |
| **IIa marginally threatened** | **none, or the toes only** | **none** | inaudible / audible | **revascularize urgently** (same day, within hours) |
| IIb immediately threatened | beyond the toes, rest pain | mild to moderate | inaudible / audible | emergency revascularization |
| III irreversible | anesthetic | paralysis, rigor | inaudible / inaudible | amputation |

**IIa is salvageable with a little time**: time to image if needed and to choose the best tool, but not to wait overnight. Re-examine sensation and movement every hour: any new numbness above the toes or weakness makes it IIb.

> **Evidence:** Rutherford 1997 categories as adopted by ESVS 2020 (Björck et al.).

### Question

**Q:** A cold, pulseless foot: the patient feels light touch on the toes and moves the ankle and toes normally. Arterial Doppler absent, venous present. Grade?
- [x] Rutherford IIa
  > No (or toe-only) sensory loss and no weakness with an inaudible arterial signal is IIa, marginally threatened: urgent revascularization.
- [ ] Rutherford I
- [ ] Rutherford IIb
- [ ] Rutherford III

## [iia-anatomy] The femoral bifurcation to the trifurcation

The **common femoral artery** lies at the mid-inguinal point and divides 3–5 cm lower into the **superficial femoral** (down the adductor canal to the hiatus) and the **profunda femoris**. The **popliteal artery** lies deep in the popliteal fossa, then divides into the **anterior tibial** (through the interosseous membrane into the anterior compartment, becoming the dorsalis pedis) and the **tibioperoneal trunk** (posterior tibial, behind the medial malleolus, and peroneal).

A **single transverse femoral arteriotomy** reaches all of these with a Fogarty catheter: proximally into the iliac, distally down the superficial femoral and profunda, and to the trifurcation.

## [iia-case] A cold foot, still moving and feeling

A **46-year-old woman** with rheumatic mitral stenosis and AF, INR 1.3 (warfarin stopped 3 weeks ago). Sudden pain and coldness of the right leg **4 hours** ago. Right foot pale and cool; **no femoral pulse**; left leg normal. **Light touch and pinprick normal; she moves her toes and ankle fully.** No arterial Doppler signal at the ankle; venous signal present.

### Question

**Q:** What is the best treatment?
- [x] Heparin, then femoral Fogarty embolectomy today
  > An abrupt onset, AF, a normal opposite leg and a lost femoral pulse mean an embolus at the femoral bifurcation in a healthy artery: balloon embolectomy is the standard treatment. IIa allows imaging but must not wait overnight.
- [ ] Catheter-directed thrombolysis over the next 24–48 hours
- [ ] Heparin alone and reassess the leg tomorrow morning
- [ ] Primary above-knee amputation

## [iia-decide] Rutherford IIa: what is the best therapy?

-   **Heparin at once**: unfractionated heparin 70–100 IU/kg (or 5000 IU) bolus, then an infusion by aPTT
-   **Imaging if it does not delay treatment**: CTA is first-line. In IIa there is usually time for it; a clear embolus (AF, normal other leg) can go to theatre on clinical grounds
-   Then match the tool to the cause:

|  | **Embolus** | **Thrombosis in situ** (or a blocked graft or stent) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clues | Sudden onset; AF, mitral stenosis, recent MI; **normal pulses in the other leg**; no claudication before | Claudication before; diseased arteries; weak pulses in the other leg; a previous bypass |
| Best therapy | **Surgical balloon (Fogarty) embolectomy**, usually through the femoral bifurcation under local or regional anesthesia; completion angiogram | **Catheter-directed thrombolysis** (an alternative to surgery in IIa) or percutaneous thrombectomy, then treat the culprit lesion; or bypass |
| Watch for | Residual clot in the tibial vessels (completion imaging) | Bleeding (major bleeding about 8–10% with thrombolysis); thrombolysis takes 24–48 hours, too slow for IIb with motor loss |

-   **Completion angiography** after open or endovascular treatment
-   Fasciotomy is **not routine** in IIa: short ischemia, no motor loss. Examine the compartments after reperfusion and open them if they are tense or painful on stretch
-   Intravenous (systemic) thrombolysis has no place

> **Evidence:** ESVS 2020: heparin while awaiting revascularization (Rec 9, I-C); imaging recommended if it does not delay treatment (Rec 5, I-C); CTA first-line (Rec 6, I-B); catheter-directed thrombolysis as an alternative to surgery in Rutherford IIa (Rec 24, I-A); IV thrombolysis not recommended (Rec 22, III-A); completion angiography (Rec 18, I-C); balloon thrombo-embolectomy is the standard treatment of embolic ALI. Endovascular Today 2026: heparin 70–100 IU/kg or 5000 IU; open embolectomy optimal for acute occlusion of relatively normal arteries; CDT takes 24–48 h with 8–10% major bleeding.

### Question

**Q:** A man with a femoropopliteal vein bypass done 2 years ago has a cold foot for 10 hours, normal sensation and movement, no Doppler signal. CTA shows an occluded graft. Best option?
- [x] Catheter-directed thrombolysis, then fix the culprit stenosis
  > A thrombosed graft in Rutherford IIa with time available suits catheter-directed thrombolysis (ESVS Rec 24, I-A), which also reveals the culprit lesion to fix.
- [ ] Fogarty embolectomy through the groin, with no imaging of the graft
- [ ] Intravenous alteplase on the ward with heparin
- [ ] Above-knee amputation, as the limb is not viable

## [ali-iia-consent] Consent: what to discuss with this patient

**1\. The patient's own risk**: The cause (embolus or thrombosis), the heart (AF, valve, recent MI), renal function, time since onset. Quote the figure, not a textbook average.

#### 2\. Common

-   Groin wound problems
-   Swelling of the leg after the blood returns

#### 3\. Serious

-   Clot left behind needing more surgery or thrombolysis
-   Compartment syndrome needing fasciotomy
-   Bleeding (more with thrombolysis)
-   Kidney injury from reperfusion or contrast
-   Loss of the leg
-   Death (the heart is often the cause)

#### 4\. Specific to this operation

-   Lifelong anticoagulation afterwards, because the source is usually the heart
-   Echo and a search for the source

#### 5\. Alternatives

-   Thrombolysis (for thrombosis in situ)
-   Amputation if the leg cannot be saved
-   No operation, and what that means

#### 6\. Recovery

-   Walking within days if the leg recovers
-   Follow-up for the heart and the anticoagulation

#### 7\. Kenya-specific

-   Warfarin needs INR checks: plan where and how she will afford them
-   Rheumatic AF: warfarin, not a DOAC

- [x] Most emboli come from the heart and recur without it
  > Recurrent embolism is common when the cardiac source is not treated; warfarin is the standard in rheumatic AF.

## [iia-expose] Expose the common femoral bifurcation; control; transverse arteriotomy

Under **local anesthesia** (often enough in IIa; anesthetist on standby), a vertical groin incision over the femoral artery. Control the **common femoral, superficial femoral and profunda** with slings. Heparin is already running. A **transverse arteriotomy** just above the bifurcation; longitudinal if the artery is diseased and will need a patch.

## [iia-fogarty] Fogarty embolectomy: proximal, then distal; check back-bleeding

The embolus often extrudes. **Proximally**: a 5F Fogarty catheter into the iliac until pulsatile inflow returns. **Distally**: a 3–4F catheter down the superficial femoral and the profunda, passed beyond the clot, the balloon inflated gently with saline and withdrawn steadily while the inflation is adjusted to the vessel. Repeat until **two clean passes** and good **back-bleeding**. Flush with heparinized saline.

Over-inflation damages the intima, causing dissection and later stenosis. If the catheter will not pass or flow stays poor, image on the table (angiogram) and explore the below-knee popliteal to direct the catheter into each tibial artery.

## [iia-clear] Clot out; close the arteriotomy; check the foot

Inflow: brisk pulsatile flow after the proximal pass. Back-bleeding from the superficial femoral and profunda is not proof of a clear run-off: **completion angiography** on the table. Close the arteriotomy, release the clamps and check the foot: color, capillary refill, Doppler signals at the ankle.

> **Evidence:** ESVS 2020 Rec 18 (I-C): completion angiography after open or endovascular treatment of ALI.

## [iia-compartments] Fasciotomy: not routine in IIa, but look

With short ischemia and no motor loss, **prophylactic fasciotomy is not needed**. After reperfusion, examine the leg hourly for 24 hours: **pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch**, a tense swollen calf, numbness in the first web space (deep peroneal nerve). Any of these: a four-compartment fasciotomy through two incisions, without delay.

How to do it: [four-compartment fasciotomy](#approach=ali-emb&step=7).

> **Evidence:** Endovascular Today 2026: compartment syndrome in up to 30% of revascularizations; prophylactic fasciotomy for severe ischemia over 6 hours; otherwise close compartment checks.

## [ali-iia-icu] ICU and post-operative care

Start with the [vascular core](#approach=cticu-vascular&step=0), the [lab schedule](#approach=cticu-core&step=0) and the [escalation table](#approach=cticu-core&step=2); then this operation's own points.

#### Specific to this operation

-   Leg checks hourly for 24 hours: pulses or Doppler signals, color, sensation, movement, compartments
-   Reperfusion: potassium, CK, creatinine and urine color (myoglobin); keep urine output up
-   Continue heparin after surgery unless there is bleeding, then **long-term anticoagulation**
-   **Rheumatic AF or mitral stenosis: warfarin (INR 2–3)**. Do not use rivaroxaban: it was worse than a vitamin K antagonist in INVICTUS
-   **Find the source**: ECG, echocardiography (left atrial thrombus, valve, LV function); if no cardiac source, image the aorta and screen for thrombophilia or cancer in the young
-   HIV test if no other cause in a young patient <span class="tag prop">proposed</span>

#### Labs

-   Day 1: FBC, UEC, CK, K⁺, coagulation; repeat K⁺ and CK at 6–12 hours

Doses: [electrolytes](#approach=cticu-doses&step=0), [vasoactive drugs](#approach=cticu-doses&step=1).

> **Evidence:** INVICTUS (Connolly et al., NEJM 2022; 4531 patients): rivaroxaban 8.2% vs VKA 6.5% for the primary composite, with more ischemic strokes and vascular deaths; VKA remains standard in rheumatic AF. Endovascular Today 2026: echo for new AF; full aortic imaging if aortic source suspected; thrombophilia or cancer work-up in selected patients.
