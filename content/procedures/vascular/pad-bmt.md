## [pad-patho] Peripheral arterial disease: a marker of the whole arterial tree

**Atherosclerosis of the leg arteries** narrows or blocks the inflow; at rest the collaterals cope, on walking the muscle outgrows its supply and becomes ischemic: **claudication**, cramping pain in the muscle below the block, relieved by standing still. When even resting flow fails: **rest pain, ulcers, gangrene** (chronic limb-threatening ischemia, CLTI).

Chain: Smoking, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CKD, age → Plaque in the leg arteries → Exercise outstrips supply → **Claudication** → Rest pain, tissue loss (CLTI)

**The same plaque is in the coronary and carotid arteries.** A claudicant is far more likely to have a heart attack or stroke than to lose the leg: the first aim of treatment is to keep the patient alive, the second to let them walk. ACC/AHA 2024 describes four clinical subsets: **asymptomatic, chronic symptomatic (claudication), CLTI and acute limb ischemia**.

In Kenya also think of **HIV-associated vasculopathy** (young, occlusive or aneurysmal, presents late) and **Takayasu arteritis** in young women.

> **Evidence:** ACC/AHA 2024 (Gornik et al.): four clinical subsets; PAD care is aimed at major adverse cardiovascular and limb events. ESVS 2024 (Nordanstig et al.): claudication rarely progresses to limb loss; cardiovascular risk dominates. HIV vasculopathy: Robbs and Paruk 2010.

**Q:** What is the commonest cause of death in a patient with intermittent claudication?
- [x] Myocardial infarction or stroke
  > PAD is a marker of systemic atherosclerosis; cardiovascular events, not limb loss, dominate the prognosis. That is why every patient needs best medical therapy.
- [ ] Sepsis from an infected, gangrenous foot
- [ ] Ruptured aortic aneurysm
- [ ] Pulmonary embolism

## [pad-anat] Where is the block? Read it from the pain

| Level of disease | Where it hurts on walking | Pulses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aorto-iliac (inflow) | Buttock, hip and thigh; erectile dysfunction (Leriche) | Weak or absent femoral pulses |
| Femoropopliteal (the commonest: the superficial femoral in the adductor canal) | **Calf** | Femoral present, popliteal and foot pulses absent |
| Tibial (diabetes, renal failure) | Foot (often silent until ulcer or gangrene; neuropathy masks pain) | Popliteal present, foot pulses absent |

Claudication is felt **one level below** the block, in the muscle supplied beyond it. Treat the **inflow before the outflow** if both are diseased.

## [pad-case] Calf pain at 150 meters

A **58-year-old matatu driver**, smokes 15 cigarettes a day, type 2 diabetes for 8 years, blood pressure 156/94. Right calf pain after about **150 m**, gone within 2 minutes of standing. No rest pain, no ulcers. Right femoral pulse present; popliteal and foot pulses absent. **ABI 0.62 on the right**, 0.95 on the left. LDL 3.8 mmol/L, HbA1c 8.9%.

### Question

**Q:** What is the first-line management?
- [x] Best medical therapy and a structured walking program
  > Claudication without CLTI is treated medically first; revascularization is considered only if symptoms remain lifestyle-limiting despite therapy and exercise.
- [ ] Femoropopliteal bypass with reversed saphenous vein this month
- [ ] Angioplasty and stenting of the superficial femoral artery
- [ ] Pentoxifylline and review in one year

## [pad-abi] The ankle-brachial index: how to do it, how to read it

Patient supine for 5–10 minutes. With a cuff and an 8 MHz hand-held Doppler, take the systolic pressure in **both brachial arteries**, then the **dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial** of each leg.

**ABI for each leg = the higher ankle pressure of that leg ÷ the higher of the two brachial pressures.**

| ABI | Meaning (ACC/AHA 2024) |
| --- | --- |
| 1.00–1.40 | Normal |
| 0.91–0.99 | Borderline |
| **0.90 or less** | **PAD** |
| Over 1.40 | **Non-compressible** (calcified: diabetes, renal failure): measure the **toe-brachial index**; 0.70 or less is PAD |

-   Normal resting ABI but typical symptoms: an **exercise ABI** (a fall after walking confirms PAD)
-   ABI does not diagnose CLTI: in suspected CLTI use toe pressures, TcPO₂ and imaging
-   Imaging (duplex, CTA) only when it will change management, usually when revascularization is being planned

> **Evidence:** ACC/AHA 2024 via AAFP 2025: ABI ≤0.90 abnormal, 0.91–0.99 borderline, 1.00–1.40 normal, >1.40 non-compressible; TBI ≤0.70 diagnostic; ABI and TBI are not useful to diagnose CLTI.

## [pad-drugs] Best medical therapy: the drugs and the targets

| What | How | Target or note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stop smoking** | Advice at every visit; **varenicline** (or bupropion, nicotine replacement); no tobacco in any form | The single most effective treatment for the leg |
| **Antiplatelet** | Single agent: **clopidogrel 75 mg** (preferred in symptomatic PAD) or aspirin 75–100 mg | Lifelong |
| **Low-dose rivaroxaban** | **2.5 mg twice daily with aspirin 75–100 mg**, if bleeding risk is low | Fewer MACE and major limb events (COMPASS; VOYAGER PAD after revascularization) |
| **High-intensity statin** | Atorvastatin 40–80 mg or rosuvastatin 20–40 mg; add ezetimibe (then a PCSK9 inhibitor) if not at target | **LDL under 1.4 mmol/L (55 mg/dL) and a fall of at least 50%** (ESVS 2024) |
| **Blood pressure** | ACE inhibitor or ARB first | Under 130/80 mmHg |
| **Diabetes** | Metformin; add an **SGLT2 inhibitor** or **GLP-1 agonist** for cardiovascular benefit | Individualized HbA1c; daily foot checks |
| **Cilostazol** (for claudication) | 100 mg twice daily; benefit judged at 3 months | **Contraindicated in heart failure**; improves walking distance, not survival |

-   Vaccination (influenza, pneumococcal) and foot care are part of the package
-   Kenya: aspirin, clopidogrel, atorvastatin, ACE inhibitors and metformin are available as lower-cost generics; rivaroxaban, ezetimibe, SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 agonists may not be affordable: prioritize stopping smoking, an antiplatelet and a statin <span class="tag prop">proposed</span>

> **Evidence:** ACC/AHA 2024: single antiplatelet (clopidogrel), rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily with low-dose aspirin for those not at increased bleeding risk, high-intensity statin, antihypertensive and diabetes therapy, smoking cessation (AHA top things to know; AAFP 2025). ESVS 2024: LDL <1.4 mmol/L and ≥50% reduction; varenicline first-line. COMPASS (Lancet 2018), VOYAGER PAD (NEJM 2020). Cilostazol reduces claudication symptoms (ACC/AHA 2024); it is contraindicated in heart failure (US label).

### Question

**Q:** A claudicant has heart failure with an ejection fraction of 30%. Which drug for claudication must be avoided?
- [x] Cilostazol
  > Cilostazol, a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor, is contraindicated in heart failure of any severity.
- [ ] Atorvastatin
- [ ] Clopidogrel
- [ ] Ramipril

## [pad-exercise] Structured exercise: the treatment that works for the walking

**Supervised exercise therapy**: walking (treadmill or track) to **moderate or near-maximal claudication pain**, rest until it settles, walk again. **30–45 minutes, at least 3 times a week, for at least 12 weeks.** Walking distance improves by building collaterals and muscle efficiency.

-   Where supervised programs are not available, a **structured home or community walking program** with a target and a log
-   Review at 3 months: walking distance, ABI, quality of life
-   Combined with best medical therapy, exercise is the standard of care before any revascularization for claudication

> **Evidence:** ACC/AHA 2024 via AAFP 2025: structured exercise at least 3 times a week for 12 weeks improves walking distance, function and quality of life. ESVS 2024 claudication guideline (Nordanstig et al.).

## [pad-revasc] When to refer for revascularization

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Asymptomatic PAD | Best medical therapy only: **never revascularize to prevent progression** |
| Claudication | Best medical therapy and exercise for at least 3 months; revascularize only if **still lifestyle-limiting**, by shared decision, with favorable anatomy |
| **CLTI** (rest pain, ulcer, gangrene) | **Prompt vascular referral**: imaging and revascularization to save the limb; WIfI staging; multidisciplinary foot care |
| **Acute limb ischemia** | Heparin and emergency referral: [Rutherford IIa](#approach=ali-iia&step=0), [IIb](#approach=ali-emb&step=0) |

The operations: [aorto-iliac](#approach=aiod-abf&step=0), [femoropopliteal bypass](#approach=fp-gsv&step=0), [amputation levels](#approach=amp-levels&step=0).

> **Evidence:** ACC/AHA 2024: revascularization prevents limb loss in CLTI; for claudication only if lifestyle-limiting despite therapy. ESVS 2024: individualized revascularization for claudication; none for asymptomatic disease. Global Vascular Guidelines 2019 for CLTI (WIfI).

## [pad-counsel] What to tell the patient, and follow-up

-   This is a disease of all the arteries: the tablets are for the heart and brain as much as the leg, and are for life
-   Stopping smoking is the most important thing you can do for your leg
-   Walk every day, through the pain to a strong ache, rest, then walk again
-   Check your feet every day; any wound, color change or pain at night: come back the same week
-   Follow-up: every 6–12 months, with ABI, blood pressure, lipids, HbA1c, smoking status and adherence
