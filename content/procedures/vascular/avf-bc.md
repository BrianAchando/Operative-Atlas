## [avb-patho] Why a fistula: access for haemodialysis

Haemodialysis needs blood flow of about 300–400 mL/min, three times a week, for years. A **fistula** joins an artery to a superficial vein: the vein **arterialises** (dilates and thickens) over weeks until it can be needled. Compared with a **graft** (PTFE) it has fewer infections and interventions; compared with a **tunnelled catheter**, far less bacteraemia and central vein stenosis.

**KDOQI 2019** replaced "fistula first" with **"patient first"**: an **ESKD Life-Plan** for every patient with progressive CKD (eGFR 15–20), choosing the access that fits that patient's expected course: forearm first, distal to proximal, non-dominant arm, protecting veins (no cannulas or blood tests in the planned arm).

**Maturation**: the old **rule of 6s** (at 6 weeks: flow 600 mL/min, diameter 6 mm, no more than 6 mm deep). KDOQI 2019 defines maturity by function: dialysis with two needles in more than two-thirds of sessions over 4 consecutive weeks.

> **Evidence:** KDOQI Clinical Practice Guideline for Vascular Access: 2019 Update (Lok et al., Am J Kidney Dis 2020): ESKD Life-Plan; no absolute minimum vessel diameter (vessels under 2 mm need careful evaluation); selective pre-operative ultrasound for high-risk patients; maturation assessed at 4–6 weeks; rope-ladder cannulation preferred. Rule of 6s: earlier KDOQI guidance.

### Case

A **67-year-old diabetic man** with **eGFR 16**. Ultrasound shows radial and forearm cephalic veins **under 2 mm**, a good brachial artery and an adequate upper arm cephalic vein. His nephrologist asks whether an elbow (brachiocephalic) fistula is appropriate and how his access plan should be built.

## [avf-bc-consent] Consent: what to discuss with this patient

**1\. The patient's own risk**: Vessel quality, diabetes, age, previous lines. Quote the figure, not a textbook average.

#### 2\. Common

-   Failure to mature
-   Bruising, swelling

#### 3\. Serious

-   Steal syndrome (hand ischaemia)
-   Thrombosis
-   Infection
-   Nerve injury
-   High-output heart failure (rare, large upper-arm fistulas)

#### 4\. Specific to this operation

-   Weeks before it can be used; further procedures to help maturation

#### 5\. Alternatives

-   Graft
-   Tunnelled catheter
-   Peritoneal dialysis
-   Conservative kidney care
-   No operation, and what that means

#### 6\. Recovery

-   ICU, then ward; drains; walking from day 1
-   Return to normal activity over weeks
-   Follow-up in clinic

#### 7\. Kenya-specific

-   Protect the arm: no cannulas or blood pressure cuffs
-   Dialysis access and cost

## [av-bc] Brachiocephalic fistula at the elbow

A **transverse incision** in the antecubital fossa. The cephalic vein (or the median cubital vein, giving outflow to both cephalic and basilic) to the **brachial artery**, end to side, with an arteriotomy limited to 4–6 mm to reduce steal. The bicipital aponeurosis is divided; the median nerve lies medial to the artery.

### Question

**Q:** Six hours after a brachiocephalic fistula the hand is cold, painful and numb. Diagnosis and action?
- [x] Access-related hand ischaemia (steal): urgent assessment; ligation or a flow-reducing or DRIL procedure
  > Severe steal with neurological signs is an emergency; it is commoner with brachial fistulas, in diabetics and the elderly.
- [ ] Normal postoperative swelling: elevate the arm
- [ ] Venous hypertension
- [ ] Thrombosis of the fistula
