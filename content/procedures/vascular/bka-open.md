## [bka-patho] Why the leg is lost, and why a below-knee level

**Mechanism.** In chronic limb-threatening ischemia, atherosclerotic occlusion of the femoropopliteal and tibial arteries leaves the foot with too little flow to heal. In diabetes, **neuropathy** (no pain, repeated pressure injury) and **infection** add to ischemia; a small wound becomes an ulcer, then osteomyelitis or wet gangrene. Tissue that cannot heal, or sepsis that cannot be controlled, is the end of the road for the foot.

**Indications for a below-knee amputation (BKA)**

-   Unreconstructable CLTI with tissue loss reaching the heel, midfoot or ankle, or a failed revascularization with a non-healable foot.
-   Wet gangrene or spreading foot sepsis (a guillotine ankle amputation first, BKA once sepsis settles).
-   Uncontrollable pain at rest in a foot that cannot be salvaged.
-   Trauma, tumor or infection confined below the knee.

**Conditions for success.** Enough blood flow to heal the calf flap (a palpable popliteal pulse is a good sign; if in doubt, vascular assessment and revascularization first), infection cleared, and a patient who can use a prosthesis. **Prefer BKA over AKA when both would heal**: keeping the knee makes prosthetic walking far more likely and less tiring. **Relative contraindications**: fixed knee flexion contracture, a non-ambulant patient, ischemia or infection reaching above the planned level.

**Risk.** Cardiac and renal disease, diabetes and age drive outcome. Qaarie, Cureus 2023 (review of published series, very heterogeneous): 30-day mortality averaged about 16% after BKA and AKA alike (BKA 6.2–51.4%, AKA 12.7–21.7%); 1-year mortality about 28% after BKA and about 50% after AKA, partly because AKA patients are sicker; 5-year mortality 39–80%.

> **Evidence:** StatPearls Below-Knee Amputation; Global Vascular Guidelines 2019; Waters et al. 1976; Qaarie, Cureus 2023 (review of published series, very heterogeneous): 30-day mortality averaged about 16% after BKA and AKA alike (BKA 6.2–51.4%, AKA 12.7–21.7%); 1-year mortality about 28% after BKA and about 50% after AKA, partly because AKA patients are sicker; 5-year mortality 39–80%.

### Question

**Q:** Which patient is the best candidate for a below-knee rather than an above-knee amputation?
- [x] An ambulant diabetic with a non-healable heel ulcer and a palpable popliteal pulse
  > The calf flap can heal and the preserved knee gives the best chance of prosthetic walking.
- [ ] A bed-bound patient with a fixed knee flexion contracture who will not use a prosthesis
- [ ] A patient with gangrene reaching above the knee
- [ ] Anyone with a pulseless popliteal artery and no revascularization option, whatever the state of the calf

## [bka-case] Case: failed revascularization, non-healing heel

He walked unaided before this. Healing the heel is unlikely, and revascularization is no longer possible. Offer a **primary below-knee amputation**: the infection is confined below the ankle, the popliteal pulse is palpable, and the knee moves fully.

### Case

A **62-year-old man**, type 2 diabetes for 20 years, a non-healing heel ulcer with osteomyelitis for 4 months. A fem-pop bypass 8 months ago has occluded; angiography shows no distal target. Afebrile, glucose controlled. Popliteal pulse palpable; no pedal pulses. Full knee extension.

### Question

**Q:** What level and why?
- [x] Below-knee: the flap should heal and the knee is kept for prosthetic walking
  > The popliteal pulse predicts healing at this level; an above-knee level is held back for failure.
- [ ] An above-knee amputation, as it heals more reliably and the knee adds little for walking
- [ ] Another bypass
- [ ] A toe amputation, accepting the risk of nonhealing to keep the foot

## [bka-incision] Mark the level and the long posterior flap

Supine, a sandbag under the thigh, tourniquet optional (not in severe ischemia). Mark in this order:

| Landmark | Measurement |
| --- | --- |
| **Anterior skin incision** | about **10 to 15 cm below the tibial tubercle** |
| **Posterior flap** | drawn about **150% of the anterior flap length** (the long posterior, Burgess, flap), enough to fold over the bone without tension |
| **Proportion of the circumference** | anterior incision about two-thirds, posterior flap about one-third |
| **Anterior muscle and periosteum** | divide the skin, fascia and anterior compartment muscle square to the bone, without beveling the skin |

In ischemic limbs, handle the skin edges gently (no forceps on skin, no undermining).

> **Evidence:** StatPearls Below-Knee Amputation: incision 10–15 cm from the tibial tubercle, posterior flap 150% of the anterior; Orthobullets (transtibial technique): anterior incision about 10 cm below the tubercle, two-thirds / one-third of the circumference.

### Question

**Q:** How long is the posterior flap compared with the anterior one?
- [x] About 150% of the anterior flap length
  > The long posterior flap carries the best blood supply and folds over the bone.
- [ ] The same length, so the scar lies at the end of the stump
- [ ] Half
- [ ] No posterior flap is made; equal sagittal flaps are always used

## [bka-bone] Divide the tibia and fibula

Elevate the periosteum only as far as needed. Divide the tibia at the planned level with an oscillating saw, perpendicular to the shaft, and **bevel the anterior tibial crest at about 45°** so it does not press through the skin. Divide the **fibula at least 1 cm proximal** to the tibial cut (1 to 2 cm is usual) so it does not touch the socket or skin. Smooth the edges with a rasp; irrigate away bone dust.

| Structure | Level |
| --- | --- |
| Tibia | skin incision 10–15 cm below the tubercle; bone cut 2–3 cm proximal to the anterior skin edge |
| Anterior tibial crest | bevel at about 45° |
| Fibula | at least 1 cm shorter than the tibia |

> **Evidence:** Orthobullets (transtibial technique): tibia cut 2–3 cm proximal to the anterior skin edge, fibula at least 1 cm proximal, distal tibia beveled 45°; StatPearls Below-Knee Amputation: bone cut with an oscillating saw, corners beveled.

### Question

**Q:** Why is the fibula cut shorter than the tibia?
- [x] A long fibula is prominent and painful in the socket
  > At least 1 cm shorter keeps the fibular end clear of the skin and socket.
- [ ] To save time
- [ ] To protect the peroneal artery, which runs along the fibula and supplies the flap
- [ ] It is not cut

## [bka-vessels] Ligate the vessels, divide the nerves

Identify each pedicle: **anterior tibial** (anterior compartment, with the deep peroneal nerve), **posterior tibial** and **peroneal** (deep posterior compartment, with the tibial nerve). **Ligate each artery and vein separately** with silk, and clip bleeders.

Put each nerve (tibial, deep and superficial peroneal, sural, saphenous) under gentle traction, divide it **sharply with a fresh blade**, and let it retract proximally among the muscle, to reduce painful neuroma. Optional: a local anesthetic block of the nerve ends.

Trim the soleus and gastrocnemius so the flap is not bulky.

> **Evidence:** StatPearls Below-Knee Amputation: each artery identified and ligated, nerves sharply divided after gentle traction; Orthobullets (transtibial technique): pedicles tied with silk, nerve cut sharply as proximally as possible.

### Question

**Q:** How are the nerves handled to prevent a painful neuroma?
- [x] Gentle traction, a sharp division, and retraction into soft tissue
  > A clean cut proximal to the stump end lets the nerve retract away from pressure points.
- [ ] Ligated with the artery in one tie to reduce bleeding from the vasa nervorum
- [ ] Cauterized at the stump end, which seals the nerve and stops it regrowing
- [ ] Left long

## [bka-close] Myodesis, drain and closure

Fold the gastrocnemius-soleus flap forward over the bone. Secure the **gastrocnemius aponeurosis to the tibia** through drill holes with non-absorbable suture (myodesis) or sew it to the anterior fascia, so the flap covers the bone and stays put. Place a drain under the fascia where you expect a collection. Close the **fascia, then the skin**, without tension; if the flap is dusky or tight, remove the stitches rather than strangling it.

Dressing: a **soft padded dressing**, or a **rigid dressing** in ambulant patients. The Cochrane review (9 trials, 436 patients) could not prove either better: the evidence is very low-certainty.

> **Evidence:** StatPearls Below-Knee Amputation: gastrocnemius aponeurosis secured to the tibia through a drill hole with non-absorbable suture; Orthobullets: blunt drain under the fascia, soft dressing; Kwah et al. Cochrane 2019: rigid dressings may shorten healing, very low-certainty evidence.

### Question

**Q:** What does the evidence say about rigid versus soft dressings after a BKA?
- [x] Uncertain: very low-certainty evidence from small trials
  > Choose by the patient: skin, fall risk, and the surgeon's experience.
- [ ] Rigid dressings always heal faster and should be standard for every BKA
- [ ] Soft dressings are proven better, since rigid casts cause more wound breakdown
- [ ] Dressings make no difference to anything

## [bka-after] After a BKA: stump, knee, pain, other leg

**Wound.** Drain out when the output is low (usually in 24–48 hours); check the flap for dusky skin; sutures out at about 2 weeks (longer in ischemic limbs). A failed flap needs a **revision or a higher (AKA) level**.

**Knee.** Straight, not bent over a pillow, to prevent a **flexion contracture**; lie prone for part of the day; physiotherapy from day 1; sit with the stump supported, never dangling.

**Pain.** Multimodal analgesia (regular paracetamol, opioids, neuropathic agents as needed). Phantom pain affects about **6 in 10** people with an amputation (pooled 64%; 54% in developing countries); persistent pre-operative pain, a more proximal level and stump pain raise the risk. Treat early.

**Medical.** Glucose, nutrition, VTE prophylaxis, antiplatelet and statin for life, smoking cessation, and **protect the other foot**: examine it every day.

**Rehabilitation.** Stump shaping, then prosthesis fitting once healed, usually from about 3 to 6 months after surgery; walking is learned over months. Early review of how the patient will manage at home.

> **Evidence:** Orthobullets: wound check and sutures at 2 weeks, prosthesis fitting and rehabilitation at 3–6 months; Limakatso et al., PLOS ONE 2020: pooled phantom limb pain prevalence 64% (95% CI 60–68); lower in developing countries (54%); Global Vascular Guidelines 2019: best medical therapy, amputation-prevention teams.

### Question

**Q:** After a BKA, why is the knee kept straight?
- [x] To prevent a knee flexion contracture that stops prosthetic walking
  > A bent knee on a pillow shortens the hamstrings; start extension exercises early.
- [ ] To prevent phantom pain, which is triggered by a bent knee stretching the nerves
- [ ] To raise blood pressure
- [ ] It does not matter, as any contracture stretches out once walking begins
