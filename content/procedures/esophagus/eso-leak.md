## [leak-patho] Why anastomoses leak

The gastric conduit lives on **one artery, the right gastroepiploic**. Its tip, now at the anastomosis, is the point farthest from that artery and drains through the thinnest veins: **ischemia of the tip** is the common root of leak and of conduit necrosis. Tension, a poor staple line, and the patient (malnutrition, low albumin, neoadjuvant chemoradiation, diabetes, smoking, vascular disease) add to it.

Chain: Tip ischemia, tension, technique → Full-thickness defect → **Saliva and gastric juice into the mediastinum** → Mediastinitis, empyema, sepsis → Fistula to the airway or aorta (late)

**Definition (ECCG):** a full-thickness gastrointestinal defect involving the esophagus, anastomosis, staple line or conduit, irrespective of presentation or method of identification. It complicates roughly **1 in 9 to 1 in 5** esophagectomies.

> **Evidence:** ECCG definition (Low 2015). Incidence 11.4–21.2% and leak-related mortality 7.2–35% across series (Fabbi 2021).

### Question

**Q:** Why is the tip of the gastric conduit the part most at risk?
- [x] It is the point farthest from its only feeding artery, the right gastroepiploic
  > The conduit is perfused along the greater curvature from the right gastroepiploic artery; the tip, now at the anastomosis, is the most distal and least perfused.
- [ ] It is crushed by the circular stapler
- [ ] It is supplied by the left gastric artery, which is divided
- [ ] It lies against the aorta

## [leak-anat] Where the leak goes

An **intrathoracic anastomosis** (Ivor Lewis) sits above the azygos arch, behind the trachea and right main bronchus, beside the descending aorta. A leak spills into the **posterior mediastinum** and, through the opened mediastinal pleura, into the **right pleural space**: mediastinitis and empyema, the reason intrathoracic leaks kill more often.

A **cervical anastomosis** (McKeown, transhiatal) leaks under the neck wound and usually drains through it; it can still track down the conduit bed into the chest.

-   **Behind the trachea:** a persistent leak can erode into the membranous airway (conduit–airway fistula: coughing on swallowing, air in the drain, recurrent pneumonia)
-   **Beside the aorta:** rarely an aorto-conduit fistula, heralded by a small "sentinel" bleed before the catastrophic one
-   **In the pleura:** a layering collection at the back and base of the right chest

## [leak-case] Day 5 after an Ivor Lewis

A 62-year-old man had an Ivor Lewis esophagectomy for a lower-third squamous carcinoma after neoadjuvant chemoradiation. On day 5 he develops **new atrial fibrillation**, a temperature of 38.4 °C and a respiratory rate of 26. CRP has climbed from 150 to 240 mg/L between days 3 and 5. The right chest drain, clear yesterday, is **turbid**. He is on jejunostomy feeds.

### Question

**Q:** What is the first investigation?
- [x] CT of the chest with oral contrast
  > For suspected intrathoracic leak the ISDE consensus recommends CT with oral contrast first (84% agreement): it shows the leak and the collections that need draining.
- [ ] Barium swallow
- [ ] Repeat CRP in 24 hours
- [ ] Bedside methylene blue by mouth

## [leak-diagnose] Making the diagnosis

| Clue | Weight (ISDE 2026) |
| --- | --- |
| Change in the drain fluid (turbid, salivary, bilious, food) | **Suggestive** (92%) |
| Fever, respiratory distress, tachycardia (new AF) | Take into account (81–85%) |
| Neck wound red and swollen (cervical anastomosis) | **Suggestive** (87%) |
| CRP: follow the **trend from day 3 to 7**; white count | Useful (86–90%) |
| Drain amylase, if drains were left | Might help (74%) |

#### Which test, in which order

-   **Intrathoracic**: **CT with oral contrast first**
-   **Cervical, no local infection**: CT with oral contrast; **with local infection**: CT or simply **open the incision at the bedside**. If septic, add a CT anyway (89%): a neck leak can track into the chest
-   Leak found at endoscopy: CT with oral contrast to map the contamination (90%)
-   Leak found on CT: **endoscopy** to size the defect and judge the conduit (98% agree it assesses the conduit)
-   **Negative CT but still suspicious: endoscopy**
-   No routine screening of well patients

> **Evidence:** ISDE 2026: statements 1–12, all evidence level C (no randomized trials). Fabbi 2021: a CRP around 17 mg/dL (170 mg/L) on day 3 marks a higher risk of leak.

### Question

**Q:** After a McKeown esophagectomy, a patient on day 6 has a red, swollen neck wound with pus, and is septic. Next?
- [x] Open the neck wound at the bedside and also get a CT with oral contrast
  > With local signs, opening the incision is acceptable first; in a septic patient the ISDE panel recommends a CT as well (89%), because a neck leak can extend into the mediastinum.
- [ ] Contrast swallow only
- [ ] Antibiotics and review in 48 hours
- [ ] Return to theatre for a thoracotomy

## [leak-decide] Grade it, then match the treatment

| ECCG type | Leak | Conduit necrosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Local defect needing no change in therapy, or only medical treatment or diet change | Focal, seen at endoscopy: monitoring or non-surgical therapy |
| II | Needs **intervention but not surgery** (radiological drain, stent, bedside opening and packing of the incision) | Focal, without free leak: surgery **not** involving diversion |
| III | Needs **surgery** | **Extensive**: conduit resection with diversion |

#### The ISDE algorithm

| Situation | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| **Every leak** | Supportive care: broad-spectrum antibiotics, feeding support, nil by mouth, nasogastric decompression |
| Confirmed leak, **no collection**, mild signs | Supportive care alone may be enough |
| **Mediastinal or pleural collection** | **Drain it** (plus supportive care); endoscopic closure (EVT, stent, or both) can be added |
| **Uncontrolled sepsis** or failure of primary treatment | **Surgical washout and drainage**; closure with sutures or tissue may be considered |
| Limited ischemia of the conduit tip, not septic | Continuity-preserving treatment |
| **Substantial conduit necrosis** | **Primary diversion** |

> **Evidence:** ECCG types: Low 2015; necrosis types as tabulated by Athanasiou 2019. Algorithm: ISDE 2026 statements 13–23 (consensus ≥80% except where stated).

## [eso-leak-consent] Consent: what to discuss with this patient

**1\. The patient's own risk**: How septic, the size of the defect and the state of the conduit at endoscopy, nutrition. Quote the figure, not a textbook average.

#### 2\. Common

-   Several procedures: endoscopy and sponge change every few days, or a stent and its removal
-   Weeks without eating by mouth; feeding through the jejunostomy
-   Drains for weeks

#### 3\. Serious

-   Spreading sepsis needing a return to theatre
-   Bleeding (sponge, stent erosion)
-   Fistula to the airway
-   Loss of the conduit and a neck stoma (diversion)
-   Death

#### 4\. Specific to this treatment

-   Stent: migration, chest pain, erosion; it must come out
-   Narrowing (stricture) of the anastomosis after healing, needing dilatation
-   After a diversion, reconstruction is a further major operation months later

#### 5\. Alternatives

-   Drainage and supportive care alone (small, contained leaks)
-   Surgery first, if sepsis is not controlled
-   Comfort care, and what that means

#### 6\. Recovery

-   Long stay: weeks, sometimes months
-   Oral intake only after the leak has healed (endoscopy or CT)
-   Follow-up for stricture

#### 7\. Kenya-specific

-   Cost of repeated endoscopy, sponges and stents; what is available here, and when transfer is better
-   Nutrition: the jejunostomy feed the family will need to buy and give

**Q:** Why must a stent placed for a leak be discussed as temporary?
- [x] Covered stents migrate and can erode; they are removed or exchanged, usually by 4–8 weeks
  > Fully covered stents are left in for a median of 4–8 weeks and then removed; leaving them risks erosion, fistula and bleeding.
- [ ] They dissolve
- [ ] Because they block the airway
- [ ] They are not: stents are permanent

## [leak-support] Supportive care for every leak

-   **Nil by mouth** (84%)
-   **Broad-spectrum antibiotics** (99%), chosen by your unit's antibiogram; consider **antifungal** cover (74%, near consensus)
-   **Feeding support** (98%): the jejunostomy, or a nasojejunal tube placed at endoscopy; parenteral only if the gut cannot be used
-   **Nasogastric decompression** of the conduit (76%): placed or repositioned **under endoscopic vision**, never blind
-   Continue a **proton pump inhibitor** (79%)

A confirmed leak with **no collection and only mild signs** can be treated this way alone. Anything more needs source control.

> **Evidence:** ISDE 2026 statements 13–15. Conservative treatment of intrathoracic leaks: pooled success 82%, mortality 9%, but a mean of 70 days to heal (Mucha 2025, 133 patients).

## [leak-drainage] Drain the collections

**Any mediastinal or pleural collection is drained** (91–96%) as well as the supportive care.

| Where | How (ISDE 2026) |
| --- | --- |
| Mediastinal collection | **EVT** through the defect (82%), a **naso-mediastinal tube**, or **radiological** drainage (81%) |
| Pleural collection | **Radiological** tube drainage (81%) or a surgical chest drain (79%) |
| Neck | Open the cervical incision at the bedside (81%) |

**Naso-mediastinal (naso-fistula) tube drainage** uses only a tube placed endoscopically through the defect into the cavity, on suction or with irrigation: cheap and widely available.

> **Evidence:** Mucha 2025 (38 studies, 899 patients, all observational): naso-fistula tube drainage success 94% and mortality 5% (201 patients), versus EVT 82% and 11%, stents 75% and 14%; the authors could not recommend one treatment over another.

### Question

**Q:** CT shows a 5 cm mediastinal collection and a right pleural collection after an Ivor Lewis. The patient is stable. Best plan?
- [x] Supportive care plus drainage of both collections, with endoscopic drainage or closure of the defect
  > Collections must be drained (ISDE 91–96%); endoscopic closure (EVT, stent) can be added. Surgery is for uncontrolled sepsis or failure.
- [ ] Supportive care alone
- [ ] Immediate rethoracotomy and redo anastomosis
- [ ] Stent alone, without drains

## [leak-neck] The cervical leak: open the wound

Remove the skin sutures over the leak, open the wound down to the collection with a finger, wash it out and **pack it**; repack daily. Most cervical leaks then heal without further intervention, and the patient can often swallow saliva into the dressing.

-   If septic, or not settling: **CT**, because the leak may extend into the mediastinum
-   Endoscopy to check the conduit if there is any doubt about its viability
-   Watch for a stricture later: cervical leaks often heal with one

> **Evidence:** ISDE 2026 statements 7, 8 and 17 (bedside opening 81%).

## [leak-evt] Endoscopic vacuum therapy (EVT)

An open-pore polyurethane sponge on a tube is placed endoscopically **through the defect into the cavity** (intracavitary) or **across the defect in the lumen** (intraluminal), and the tube brought out through the nose to **continuous suction of −100 to −125 mmHg**. It drains the cavity, removes the infected fluid and draws the walls together. The sponge is changed **every 3–4 days** (up to a week if intraluminal); the cavity shrinks with each change.

-   Typical course: about 4–5 sponges over 2–3 weeks
-   Complications: bleeding, sponge displacement, fistula
-   **Without a commercial kit:** a sponge cut from wound-vacuum foam, tied to a nasogastric tube and pushed into place over a guidewire, on a standard wound-vacuum pump at 125 mmHg <span class="tag prop">proposed</span>

> **Evidence:** ISDE 2026 statement 20: EVT 82%, stent 71%, EVT with stent 77%. Fabbi 2021: −100 to −125 mmHg, change every 3–4 days, success 86–100% for intrathoracic leaks. Mucha 2025: mean 4.5 sponges, 19 days, success 82%, reintervention 26%. Home-made sponges: Saraiva 2020 (foam on a nasogastric tube, changed every 3–4 days), Chen 2025 (wound-vac foam, 125 mmHg).

### Question

**Q:** What suction is applied to an esophageal EVT sponge?
- [x] Continuous −100 to −125 mmHg
  > The usual EVT setting is continuous negative pressure of about −100 to −125 mmHg, with sponge changes every 3–4 days.
- [ ] Intermittent −20 mmHg
- [ ] Free drainage only
- [ ] −400 mmHg wall suction

## [leak-stent] Covered stent

A **fully covered self-expanding metal stent** is placed across the anastomosis, landing about 4 cm above and below the defect, to seal it from the lumen. It lets the patient swallow early, but it does **not drain** a collection: drain any collection as well.

-   Works best for a **small defect** found **early**
-   Migration: higher with plastic stents, lower with fully covered metal ones; check its position on X-ray
-   Erosion into the airway or aorta, bleeding
-   **Remove or exchange by 4–8 weeks**
-   EVT and a stent can be combined (a stent with a sponge built in, or sequentially)

> **Evidence:** ISDE 2026 statement 20 (stent 71%, combined 77%). Fabbi 2021: median stenting 4–8 weeks; smaller defects and earlier diagnosis predict success. Mucha 2025: stents 75% success, 14% mortality, 21% reintervention.

## [leak-reop] Surgery for uncontrolled sepsis

If sepsis is **not controlled**, or drainage and endoscopic treatment have failed: **reopen the right chest, wash out and drain** (84–85%). Decorticate the lung if the empyema has organised. If the defect is small and the tissues healthy, it can be **closed with sutures** and buttressed with tissue (an intercostal muscle or omental flap): a weak recommendation (IIb).

-   Re-look at the conduit: dusky or black means necrosis, which changes the operation to a diversion
-   Leave wide drains near the defect; place a feeding jejunostomy if there is none

> **Evidence:** ISDE 2026 statements 18 and 21.

## [leak-necrosis] Conduit necrosis and diversion

At endoscopy the mucosa of the conduit tip is **dusky, black or sloughed**. Limited ischemia in a patient who is not septic: **preserve continuity** (drain, EVT, close watch). **Substantial necrosis: primary diversion**, whether the patient is septic or not.

#### Diversion

-   Resect the necrotic conduit; return the viable stomach to the abdomen or resect it
-   Bring the esophageal remnant out as a **cervical esophagostomy** (spit fistula) on the left neck
-   Drain the chest widely; feeding jejunostomy
-   Reconstruction later, when the patient has recovered, usually with colon

> **Evidence:** ISDE 2026 statements 22 (84%) and 23 (89%). ECCG conduit necrosis type III: extensive, treated with conduit resection and diversion.

- [ ] Covered stent across the anastomosis

## [eso-leak-icu] ICU and post-operative care

Start with the [thoracic core](#approach=cticu-thoracic&step=0), the [lab schedule](#approach=cticu-core&step=0) and the [escalation table](#approach=cticu-core&step=2); then this treatment's own points.

#### Specific to the leak

-   Sepsis: cultures (blood, drain, pleural fluid) before antibiotics; narrow them on the results
-   Drains: volume and character daily. Saliva, food or bile in the drain means the leak is not controlled
-   CRP every 1–2 days: a falling trend is the best sign of control; if not falling, **repeat CT** for an undrained collection
-   EVT: keep the suction continuous, check the seal and the canister; book the sponge change every 3–4 days
-   Stent: chest X-ray for position; new chest pain or a fall in hemoglobin means migration or erosion
-   **Feeding**: full enteral feeding through the jejunostomy; refeeding precautions in the malnourished
-   **Red flags**: coughing on swallowing saliva or air in the drain (airway fistula); **any fresh blood from the NG or drain** (sentinel bleed of an aorto-conduit fistula: CT angiography now)
-   Oral intake only after healing is shown (endoscopy or contrast study)

#### Labs

-   FBC, UEC, CRP, albumin, Mg, PO₄ at least every 48 hours while the leak is active

Doses: [electrolytes](#approach=cticu-doses&step=0), [vasoactive drugs](#approach=cticu-doses&step=1).

### Question

**Q:** Ten days into EVT, the patient's CRP has risen again and the drain shows food particles. Next?
- [x] Repeat CT with oral contrast to look for an undrained collection, and re-endoscope
  > A rising CRP with gastrointestinal content in the drain means the leak is not controlled; image for collections and reassess the defect and conduit.
- [ ] Stop antibiotics
- [ ] Start oral feeding
