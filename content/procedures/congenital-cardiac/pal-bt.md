## [bt-patho] Why a shunt: too little pulmonary blood flow

A systemic-to-pulmonary shunt **adds pulmonary blood flow** when the RV outflow cannot: tetralogy with small PAs or in a sick infant, pulmonary atresia, critical pulmonary stenosis, tricuspid atresia and other single-ventricle circulations.

Chain: Too little flow to the lungs → Cyanosis, acidosis (or duct-dependent: prostaglandin E1) → **PTFE tube from a systemic artery to a PA** → Saturations about 75–85%, PAs grow

The balance is delicate: too small a shunt leaves the baby blue; too big floods the lungs and steals diastolic flow from the coronaries and the gut.

### Question

**Q:** A shunt that is too large for the baby causes what?
- [x] Pulmonary over-circulation with diastolic steal, causing coronary and gut ischemia
  > Blood runs off into the lungs throughout diastole; coronary perfusion and mesenteric flow fall.
- [ ] Worsening cyanosis, because high shunt flow raises pulmonary pressure and reverses the shunt right-to-left
- [ ] Hypertension
- [ ] Pulmonary oligemia and a falling saturation as the shunt competes with the native outflow tract

## [bt-anatomy] Innominate artery, right PA and the nerves

**KNH practice: median sternotomy**, with a graft from the **innominate artery or the right subclavian artery** to the **right pulmonary artery**; the exact technique varies by consultant. The right PA is reached medial to the SVC, between the SVC and the ascending aorta. Through a thoracotomy (classical), from the subclavian artery to the ipsilateral PA. Protect the **phrenic** and **vagus** nerves (and the recurrent laryngeal nerve hooking the subclavian on the right).

## [bt-case] Case: a blue 6-week-old

Tetralogy with very small PAs, too small and sick for complete repair: a **modified BT shunt** now, repair later.

### Case

A **6-week-old, 3.4 kg**, increasingly cyanosed (SpO₂ 62%), poor feeding. Echo: tetralogy with severe infundibular stenosis, a hypoplastic annulus and **branch PAs of 3 mm**; the duct has closed.

### Question

**Q:** Shunt size for a 3.4 kg baby?
- [x] 3.5 mm PTFE
  > About 1 mm per kg in small infants: 3–3.5 mm for 3–4 kg; 4 mm for larger infants. Oversizing raises the risk of death from over-circulation.
- [ ] 6 mm PTFE, to allow for growth
- [ ] 2 mm
- [ ] 5 mm PTFE, to limit thrombosis

## [bt-decision] Shunt size, route and risk

| Point | Practice |
| --- | --- |
| Size by weight | About 1–1.2 mm per kg in neonates and small infants: **3 mm** under about 3 kg, **3.5 mm** about 3–4 kg, **4 mm** for bigger infants; older children larger (consultant and unit practice). Use the calculator below |
| Check the PA | The graft should not be bigger than the branch PA it feeds; a small PA with a big graft floods one lung |
| Route | **KNH: sternotomy**, innominate or right subclavian artery to the right PA (bypass on standby, easy to take down at repair); thoracotomy elsewhere |
| Alternatives | Ductal stent (duct-dependent), RVOT stent or balloon (tetralogy), early complete repair |
| Risk | Not a small operation: mortality around 9% in neonates and infants in one series; early thrombosis about 9% |

> **Evidence:** Dirks et al., EJCTS 2013 (32 shunts): sizes 3 mm 25%, 3.5 mm 59%, 4 mm 16%; median 1.21 mm/kg; mortality 9.4%, thrombosis 9.4% within 24 h; lower weight and larger shunt per kg predicted death.

## [pal-bt-consent] Consent: what to discuss with this patient

**1\. The patient's own risk**: Weight and age, diagnosis (single ventricle higher risk), shunt size per kg. Quote the figure, not a textbook average.

#### 2\. Common

-   ICU
-   Drain

#### 3\. Serious

-   Shunt thrombosis (sudden desaturation, loss of the murmur)
-   Over-circulation and low diastolic pressure
-   Phrenic nerve injury
-   Death (around 1 in 10 in some infant series)

#### 4\. Specific to this operation

-   A second operation (complete repair or next stage) is planned
-   Aspirin until the next operation

#### 5\. Alternatives

-   Ductal or RVOT stent
-   Early complete repair where anatomy allows
-   No operation, and what that means

#### 6\. Recovery

-   ICU, then ward; drains; walking from day 1
-   Return to normal activity over weeks
-   Follow-up in clinic

#### 7\. Kenya-specific

-   Access to oxygen saturation monitoring and aspirin at home

### Question

**Q:** Which complication of a BT shunt is an emergency the parents should recognize?
- [x] Shunt blockage: the baby becomes suddenly bluer
  > Thrombosis is commonest early; sudden desaturation needs immediate assessment.
- [ ] Mild cough with a runny nose in the first weeks at home
- [ ] Sweating with feeds, as many shunted babies do in the first months
- [ ] A slightly warm baby after a feed

## [bt-sternotomy] Median sternotomy

Median sternotomy; in a small child keep a **thymic remnant** (or excise part of it) and harvest a piece of **anterior pericardium** for the patch before heparin. Fix it in 0.6% glutaraldehyde for a few minutes if you want it stiffer and easier to handle.

## [bt-shunt] Sew the shunt: innominate end, then PA end

Heparin (about 100 U/kg; check the unit protocol). **Side-biting clamp** on the innominate (or right subclavian) artery; bevel the PTFE tube and sew it end-to-side with running **7-0 or 8-0 polypropylene**. Then a clamp on the right PA (watch saturations: the baby may not tolerate PA clamping), and the distal anastomosis.

Open the shunt: **saturations should rise to about 75–85%** and the **diastolic pressure fall** a little. A thrill should be felt over the tube.

## [pal-bt-icu] ICU and post-operative care

Start with the [cardiac core](#approach=cticu-cardiac&step=0), the [lab schedule](#approach=cticu-core&step=0) and the [escalation table](#approach=cticu-core&step=2); then this operation's own points.

#### Specific to this operation

-   Heparin early (unit protocol), then aspirin
-   Target saturations about 75–85%; falling saturation or loss of the shunt murmur: shunt thrombosis, an emergency (heparin, echo, theatre or cath)
-   Diastolic pressure and lactate: over-circulation; avoid hyperventilation and high oxygen, which drop PVR further
-   Children: doses by weight

#### Labs

-   Day 1 morning: FBC, UEC, LFTs, Ca, Mg, PO₄ <span class="tag knh">KNH practice</span>

Doses: [electrolytes](#approach=cticu-doses&step=0), [vasoactive drugs](#approach=cticu-doses&step=1).

> **Evidence:** Dirks 2013: early thrombosis 9.4%, mortality 9.4% in 32 infants.

### Question

**Q:** 12 h after a BT shunt, SpO₂ falls from 82% to 60% and the continuous murmur has gone. Next?
- [x] Heparin bolus, urgent echo and return to theatre or the cath lab
  > Acute shunt thrombosis is life-threatening; restore flow quickly.
- [ ] Increase oxygen and wait, since the shunt murmur often fades as pulmonary resistance falls
- [ ] Furosemide, as the desaturation reflects pulmonary edema from over-circulation
- [ ] Start propranolol
